Are people with mental illnesses more likely to commit crimes? In criminal cases with mentally ill offenders, is the mental illness the cause of the crime, or are other factors responsible? Do rehabilitation efforts for mental illness help prevent crime?

Psychologists and criminologists have studied the correlation between mental illness and crime for a long time, and the concept seems especially relevant today when we see school shootings, homicide, and sexual assault throughout the media on a near daily basis. In this blog, I want to explore the connection between mental illness and crime, look at ways to attack the root of the problem through rehabilitation, and give my opinion on the feasibility of these solutions.

## Mental Illness in Crime

In the 1960s state-funded psychiatric hospitals in the United States began to downsize, causing all but the most severe cases of mental illness patients to be dismissed from their care before significant improvements to their condition were shown. In the early 1960s, the average length of a stay in these state hospitals was 6 months, declining to an average stay of only 10 days by 2007. The average treatment for these patients became a quick diagnosis and administration of medication, without much additional oversight. This led to care for patients with severe mental illness to be diverted to the individual or their families, many being unable to properly provide consistent treatment for their condition.

With lower capacity for individuals with severe mental illness, many patients found themselves homeless and in and out of the justice system. Studies estimate that the percentage of state prison inmates with a history of mental health problems ranges from about 25% to as high as 64%. This varies widely depending on the study's definition of mental illness. Those with severe mental health issues are less likely to be released on bail, and are more likely to be abused by other inmates and prison staff. Without properly trained staff in these facilities for mentally ill inmates, this creates a very hostile environment for any inmate with these conditions. The shift in mental health patients being held in prisons rather than psychiatric hospitals has caused a vicious cycle of these people being untreated and abused.

## Causes of Crime in Mentally Ill Offenders

With as many as 64% of prison inmates having a history of mental illness, it's easy to assume that mental illness is a leading cause for criminal behavior. This doesn't show the whole picture, however, as many studies have pointed out that the root cause for criminal behavior in mentally ill patients is often due to these same individuals displaying substance-abuse issues. Many studies argue that the correlation is not that mental illness leads to violent crime, but that mental illness leads to substance abuse which then leads to violent crime. It is hard to definitively make this conclusion though, since many studies are flawed in their approach, and many studies are conducted by advocacy groups with an agenda to either downplay the risk of violence in order to reduce the stigma in mental health patients, or exaggerate the correlation between mental health and violent behavior to pressure policies to increasing funding for treatment sites. Poverty, unstable economic situations, social isolation, and a previous exposure to violence, all of which are more common in individuals suffering from mental illness, are also factors that lead to increased criminal behavior.

Because of these inconsistent conclusions, it is my intention in this blog to take a deeper look at individual mental illness diagnoses and look at their specific correlation with crime, rather than focus entirely on studies under the broad umbrella of "mental illness."

## Preventative Policies and Rehabilitation

Efforts are being taken to reduce the prevalence of mentally ill individuals with mental illness within the justic system. Most of these approaches focus on reducing repeat criminal behavior and preventing unnecessary incarceration, rather than solely treating the underlying mental health condition. One common approach is the use of police-led diversion programs, in which police officers are given additional training to be able to identify individuals experiencing psychiatric issues and connect them with medical professionals and treatment facilities rather than processing them within the justice system. Studies investigating the success of these treatments have produced mixed results. Many found short-term improvements both in symptoms and reincarceration, but there is less evidence that these programs have lasting long-term effects. Follow-ups in these studies after individuals leave care are uncommon, making it difficult to tell if improvements are sustained.
